Lifting: build muscle, protect the tendon

Running doesn't have to cost you muscle. A 43-study meta-analysis found concurrent training didn't significantly reduce hypertrophy or maximal-strength gains; the cost was explosive strength, mostly when both were done in the same session (Schumann et al., 2022). So the goal here is real hypertrophy everywhere except the quads, which are capped by the patellar tendon, not by the running.

The three levers

Volume. More weekly sets per muscle means more growth, with diminishing returns: the newest dose-response meta-regression estimates about 0.24% extra hypertrophy per set around 12 sets/week (Pelland et al., 2026). Targets below sit at 12–20 sets for upper-body muscles.

Frequency. Training each muscle at least twice a week beats once a week for hypertrophy (Schoenfeld et al., 2016). Every upper-body muscle and the posterior chain is hit twice below.

Effort. Upper-body isolation work goes to 0–1 reps in reserve (RIR), compounds 1–3 RIR. Leg work stays at 2–3 RIR to protect running quality and the tendon.

Why the quads are handled differently

Heavy slow resistance is a first-line treatment for patellar tendinopathy, so heavy squats stay, with a slow tempo. But tendon collagen shows net breakdown for 24–36 h after hard loading and net synthesis at 36–72 h, so quads get one heavy day a week. Extra quad volume comes from blood-flow-restriction (BFR) leg extensions: light loads, high metabolic stress, low tendon force. BFR also ranked near the top of a recent network meta-analysis of patellar tendinopathy exercise, though that ranking wasn't a statistically significant difference.

Tendon check, every morning: single-leg decline squat, rate pain 0–10. Pain up to about 3–5/10 during loading is fine if it's back to baseline next morning and not climbing week to week. If it's 2+ points above your baseline, swap the next run for a bike ride and drop a set from the next leg day.

Progression: double progression. Hit the top of the rep range at the target RIR, then add load. Cut sets by a third on run down weeks (4, 8, 15, 21).

Fueling the muscle: each mile run costs roughly 100–130 kcal at your size, so a 30-mile week needs about 3,000–4,000 extra kcal/week just to stand still. Stay at maintenance or a small surplus from week 13 on, and keep protein around 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day. Muscle can't grow at peak mileage in a deficit, and a deficit raises bone-stress risk. Speculative: expect clear gains in upper body, arms, glutes and calves; quad size roughly maintained until after the race.

Training for this course

Miles 3–6. Runners describe short but very steep hills here. Hill repeats in weeks 3–10 cover this. On race day, run 7:35–7:45 through this stretch.

Miles 11–20, Bay Bridge. An out-and-back to Yerba Buena Island on a steady grade that a cyclist put at about 5%. From week 13, do 1.5–2 mi sustained climbs at 4–6%. Hold effort, and accept 7:45–8:00 uphill.

Traffic noise. Several reviewers found the loud traffic on the bridge path draining. Do a few long runs next to busy roads so it isn't new on race day.

Downhill off the bridge. This is heavy eccentric load. Add controlled downhill finishes on weeks 16–23 long runs only if the next-morning tendon check stays at baseline. Build the distance slowly.

3rd Street, late. One reviewer called the end a long up-and-down slog. The MP finishes in weeks 22–23 rehearse this.

Distance. One runner's watch read 0.3 mi short against the course, and the last three mile markers were missing. Pace by effort.

Weather. Race-day mean is about 56°F, average high 62°F. Pace groups are offered.

Fueling and recovery

During the race: 60–90 g carbohydrate per hour. Glucose-only products cap absorption around 60 g/h; a 2:1 glucose:fructose blend lets you go higher. For 3:16, that's about 200–290 g total.

Gut training: start at 40–50 g/h on long runs in week 13, and reach race dose by week 19. Studies of repeated 90 g/h exposure found reduced GI symptoms, but results across the literature are mixed. Use your race-day products.

A 2025 study reported average marathoners take in only about 21.7 g/h. That's a common way to hit the wall.

Daily: keep protein around 1.6–2.2 g/kg. Finish any cut by week 12; weeks 13–25 should be at maintenance or above.

Why no deficit at peak: the IOC's 2023 REDs consensus describes low energy availability as a spectrum, from mild and transient to problematic. Prolonged exposure harms bone health and performance, and it affects men as well as women.

Carb load: 2 days out, roughly 8–10 g/kg/day of carbohydrate. Practice the load before the week 18 half.

Rules that override the table

Progression. In a cohort of 874 novice runners, those who increased weekly distance by more than 30% over two weeks were more vulnerable to distance-related injuries (patellofemoral pain, IT band syndrome, shin splints) than those who increased by less than 10%. This plan stays under that threshold relative to the last non-down week.

Pain. Pain that changes your gait, bone pain you can point to with one finger, or Achilles pain that worsens during a run: stop and take 3 days off running. Cross-train instead. If it isn't clearly better, see a sports medicine clinician.

Intensity. Recreational marathoners tend to train pyramidally: most work easy, some moderate, a little hard. A 2026 randomized trial comparing polarized and pyramidal training in recreational marathoners found different adaptations but comparable race outcomes. This plan is pyramidal, with tempo work early and MP work late.

Patellar tendon. Heavy leg work is never the night before a run. Wednesday legs are followed by no run on Thursday, giving roughly 36–40 h before Friday's easy run and 4 days before the long run. Plyometrics are out until you've had 4+ symptom-stable weeks. Rising week-over-week pain, night pain, or pain above 3–4/10 on easy runs means see a sports physiotherapist.

Missed week. Repeat the previous week instead of jumping ahead.

What people who've done it report

  • Brett Williams (Men's Health) trained for the 2022 NYC Marathon while chasing a 500 lb deadlift. His goal was 3:05; he ran 3:23:30 and spent three months rehabbing a hamstring. He calls it the "volume trap." This is why leg volume drops here.
  • Nick Bare is proof the ceiling is high: a 2:39 marathon and a 700 lb deadlift, built over more than a decade.
  • Jeremy Miller (on Zach Bitter's podcast) ran a 2:44 at Chicago and, later that year, a sub-5 mile and a 500 lb deadlift on the same day.
  • Sam Loch, an Olympic rower and powerlifter, is chasing a 300 kg deadlift and a sub-3 marathon at the same time. His one previous marathon was a 3:14.
  • A Hansons reviewer with half-Ironman experience wouldn't attempt the beginner plan's jump from about 12 to 31.5 mi/week in a month. That's why this base phase is gradual.
  • First-time Higdon users on Substack reported an 18-week plan worked, one noting she used smaller jumps between long runs.
  • A forum lifter-runner found runs felt like only 80–90% effort the day after squats and deadlifts, and moved to a full day off legs after leg day.
  • Oakland runners on RaceRaves say it isn't flat, and describe steep early hills, a draining bridge, and the 3rd Street grind.
